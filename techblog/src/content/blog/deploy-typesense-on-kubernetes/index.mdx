<Aside type="tip" title="TL;DR">
Typesense is a cracking little search engine. Running it as a highly available cluster on Kubernetes, however, is where things get interesting. Raft needs a consistent view of its peers; Kubernetes likes replacing Pods whenever it feels like it. Not exactly a match made in heaven.

I built **Typesense Kubernetes Operator (TyKO)** to handle the awkward bits: deploying Typesense, managing cluster membership, and recovering from certain quorum-loss scenarios without requiring someone to babysit the cluster. This article explains the problem, the reasoning behind the operator, and how to get a three-node cluster running with Helm and a small custom resource.
</Aside>

## Typesense: the easy part

[Typesense](https://typesense.org/) is an open-source search engine built around a fairly sensible idea: search should be fast, relevant, and not require a postgraduate degree in distributed systems just to get started.

You get **full-text search**, **real-time indexing**, **faceted filtering**, and **sorting**, along with more advanced features such as **vector and semantic search**. It's a useful alternative when you want capable application search without taking on the operational weight of something like [Elasticsearch](https://www.elastic.co/elasticsearch).

So far, so good. Then we decide to make it highly available on Kubernetes. Because apparently I enjoy making simple things distributed.

## Kubernetes and Raft: an awkward relationship

Typesense can run as a managed service through **Typesense Cloud**, or you can host it yourself using Docker, Docker Compose, or native binaries. Those options are fine, but I wanted a Kubernetes-native way to run and manage a highly available cluster.

The catch is that Typesense uses [braft](https://github.com/baidu/braft), an implementation of the [Raft consensus algorithm](https://raft.github.io/raft.pdf), to coordinate its nodes.

<GithubCard repo='baidu/braft' />

Raft needs its members to agree on who belongs to the cluster and which node is the leader. Kubernetes, meanwhile, treats Pods as replaceable. They restart, move between workers, and receive new IP addresses. If cluster membership isn't kept in sync with reality, you can end up with stale peer information, lost quorum, and an application that would very much prefer you didn't touch anything.

A StatefulSet gives Pods stable names and storage identities, but it doesn't magically solve the application's consensus and recovery problems. Helm charts and scripts can help with deployment; they don't, by themselves, provide a controller that continuously reconciles Typesense's view of its peers with the Kubernetes environment.

## A quick detour into Raft

A Raft node can be a **follower**, **candidate**, or **leader**. Followers receive replicated log entries from the leader. If a follower stops hearing from a leader, it can become a candidate and request votes. A candidate that wins a majority becomes the new leader.

The important word here is **majority**. A Raft cluster with **`N` voting members** requires a strict majority to maintain quorum. The maths isn't particularly complicated:

- **Quorum:** `floor(N / 2) + 1`
- **Failures tolerated:** `floor((N - 1) / 2)`

Assuming the remaining members can still communicate with each other, here's what that looks like:

- **1 member:** Quorum of 1, tolerates 0 failures. Not exactly what I'd call highly available.
- **3 members:** Quorum of 2, tolerates 1 failure. A reasonable starting point for most deployments.
- **5 members:** Quorum of 3, tolerates 2 failures. Better fault tolerance, but also more infrastructure to maintain.

And that's where the trouble starts. **Kubernetes manages the lifecycle of containers, 
while Raft manages distributed consensus.** Both are doing exactly what they're supposed to do, 
but neither automatically solves the other's problems.Getting them to play nicely together is a different story.

Lose too many members and the surviving nodes cannot safely elect a leader or commit new writes. That's not Raft being broken. That's Raft doing exactly what it was designed to do: preventing conflicting decisions when the cluster can't establish a majority.

Now add Kubernetes to the equation. Pods can disappear during node failures, upgrades, evictions, or the occasional self-inflicted maintenance disaster. If the application's membership configuration doesn't keep up, what should have been a routine reschedule can become a rather unpleasant recovery exercise.

<Aside type="danger">
Typesense's [high-availability documentation](https://typesense.org/docs/guide/high-availability.html#recovering-a-cluster-that-has-lost-quorum) explains why quorum loss may require manual verification and recovery. In particular, blindly bootstrapping the surviving nodes can risk split-brain behaviour if another partition is still running.

**For a search service that applications depend on, I'd rather not make manual cluster surgery part of 
the operating procedure.**
</Aside>


## Meet TyKO: Typesense Kubernetes Operator

[Typesense Kubernetes Operator](https://akyriako.github.io/typesense-operator-docs/), or **TyKO**, is my attempt to make Typesense behave like a proper Kubernetes workload without pretending that Raft doesn't exist.

Instead of juggling a collection of manifests and hoping the cluster sorts itself out, TyKO introduces a `TypesenseCluster` custom resource and a controller that manages the underlying resources and reacts to changes.

It handles:

- **Cluster deployment:** A StatefulSet, persistent volume claims, and the Services needed for discovery and health checks.
- **Configuration and credentials:** Admin API keys stored as Kubernetes Secrets, plus node configuration maintained through a ConfigMap.
- **Membership reconciliation:** Discovering Typesense nodes and updating the peer configuration as the Kubernetes workload changes, without bolting on another sidecar.
- **Exposure and observability:** Optional Ingress configuration and Prometheus metrics integration.
- **Content crawling:** [DocSearch Scraper](https://github.com/typesense/typesense-docsearch-scraper) CronJobs.
- **Recovery:** Automated handling of certain lost-quorum scenarios, rather than immediately handing the mess to an unfortunate human.

That last point deserves a caveat: **automated recovery is not a substitute for backups, reliable storage, or understanding split-brain risks**. The operator is designed to recover from scenarios it can safely handle; it cannot resurrect destroyed data or repair broken hardware by sheer force of YAML.

The goal isn't to hide the distributed-systems problem. It's to automate the repetitive operational work around it.

## Enough theory. Deploy the bloody thing.

First, install the operator using Helm:

```bash
helm repo add typesense-operator https://akyriako.github.io/typesense-operator/
helm repo update

helm upgrade --install typesense-operator typesense-operator/typesense-operator \
  -n typesense-system --create-namespace
```

Next, create a `TypesenseCluster` resource. Here's a minimal three-node example:

```yaml
apiVersion: ts.opentelekomcloud.com/v1alpha1
kind: TypesenseCluster
metadata:
  name: demo-cluster
spec:
  image: typesense/typesense:29.0
  replicas: 3
  storage:
    size: 100Mi
    storageClassName: <STORAGE_CLASS_NAME>
```

Replace `<STORAGE_CLASS_NAME>` with a StorageClass available in your cluster. The `100Mi` volume size is fine for a quick demonstration, not a sensible production capacity plan. Size your storage for your actual dataset and growth, not for the comforting illusion that disks never fill up.

The operator takes care of provisioning and managing the Typesense resources. The same approach is intended to work whether you're experimenting with **kind**, running your own bare-metal Kubernetes cluster, or using a managed Kubernetes platform.

Want to change the Typesense version? Update `spec.image`. Need more storage? Adjust `spec.storage.size`, subject to what your StorageClass and CSI driver support. As always, check version compatibility, upgrade paths, and backup requirements before doing either in production. A declarative API isn't a licence to skip the boring bits.

For the full set of options, have a look at the [TyKO CRD documentation](https://akyriako.github.io/typesense-operator-docs/docs/crds/).

## So, why an operator?

Because a StatefulSet can keep three Pods running, but it can't independently understand whether those Pods form a healthy Raft cluster.

That's the distinction. Kubernetes knows about desired replicas, volumes, and Services. Typesense knows about peers, leaders, and consensus. TyKO sits between the two and tries to keep those views aligned.

Is an operator more moving parts than a Helm chart? Absolutely. But if the alternative is a collection of scripts, sidecars, and a runbook titled *How to Rebuild Quorum at 3 AM*, I'd rather put that logic in a controller where it can be reconciled and maintained.

The project documentation is available at [akyriako.github.io/typesense-operator-docs](https://akyriako.github.io/typesense-operator-docs/), and the source lives on GitHub. If you're running TyKO, there's also an [Adopters page](https://akyriako.github.io/typesense-operator-docs/docs/adopters/) where you can make that known.
